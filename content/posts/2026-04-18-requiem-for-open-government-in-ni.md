# Farewell Clare

In a relatively non-descript office building called 'Clare House', on the end of Airport Road on the edge of Belfast, served by no Public Transport, with apparrently limited parking and no cafeteria, sits the 'heart' of the Northern Ireland Department of Finance. 

This wasn't my first time here, probably been through the Visitor Pass rigamarole about a dozen times at this point, but this one had a long-bubbling tension sitting under it.

It was a breakup. Or, I guess, more accurately, an annulment.

# Starting from Behind

I'm going to operate on the assumption that if you're reading this, you're either well aware of the terms 'Open Data', 'Open Government', or that you're capable of Googling or asking your AI agent of choice to do your thinking for you.

The Open Government Partnership was launched at the [United Nations in September 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Government_Partnership#History) as a multilateral initiative to secure concrete committments from governments to promote transparency, integrity, accountability and multi-stakeholder participation. The UK was one of the eight founding members, along with the US, Norway, Brazil, Infonesia, Mexico, the Phillipines, and South Africa. Within 6 mopnths, 54 countries had committed to participating, and 50 had declared 'National Action Plans' (NAP).

To quote a quote of a quote from [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Government_Partnership#History); (emphasis mine)

> According to the then Minister of the United Kingdom's [Cabinet Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_Office) responsible for public transparency and open data, [Francis Maude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Maude), Britain sought to "further secure the foundations of OGP as a globally  recognized and respected international initiative…. [and to] strengthen the role of **civil society organizations**, encouraging greater  collaboration with governments to forge **more innovative and open ways of working**.

This was expressed in UK's [NAP1](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-open-government-national-action-plan-2011-to-2013), which included the establishment of data.gov.uk, and led with six top level committments on 

1. Right to Data - New powers to independent organsations to secure the release of valuable public datasets
2. Setting standards - Formalising the Public Data Principales, making clear the minimum that citizens can expect on publication and quality of data
3. Corporate and Personal Responsibility - establishing 'Transparency Boards' to departments and other parts of the public sector which hold datasets of greatest value, including a industrial collaboration and expert-sharing mode to 'challenge government in making more data pubilc'
4. Collecting and publishing the right data - establishing a framework for public service providers to have common,  consistent and transparent data inventories outlining what datasets are  held, and whether they are open or not, using standards set by central  government
5. Maximise the opening up of data - routinely publish evidence behind policy statements and survey data by default
6. Stimulating the market for innovative use of open data - Public service providers to report each year on how they are building collaborative relationships with the user community around the use of public data

Northern Ireland was not mentioned once in NAP1. 'Open' was menthioned around 45 times

Now, it's worth saying (especially as the domain owner of https://howlonghasnorthernirelandnothadagovernment.com/) that Northern Ireland did infact both existing and have a government.

# Yearning for Erne

In 2013, the UK had the [Presidency of the G8](https://www.gov.uk/government/topical-events/g8-2013) (this was before Russia got all grabby and was still invited), and for some [unfathomable](https://www.thejournal.ie/northern-ireland-g8-false-fronts-947963-Jun2013/) reason, decided that Lough Erne 'Resort' in Fermanagh was the right backdrop to, amont other things, agree on an '[Open Data Charter](http://www.g8.utoronto.ca/summit/2013lougherne/lough-erne-open-data.html)', setting out five strategic priorities:

- Open Data by Default
- Quality and Quantity
- Useable by All
- Releasing Data for Improved Governance 
- Releasing Data for Innovation 

One of my *favourite* bits of the Charter is sections 3 and 4

> Access to data allows individuals and organisations to develop new  insights and innovations that can improve the lives of others and help  to improve the flow of information within and between countries. While governments and businesses collect a wide range of data, they do  not always share these data in ways that are easily discoverable,  useable, or understandable by the public. [4] This is a missed opportunity. 

This charter, in just 29 short sections, recognises both the soft and hard advantages of 'Open Data/Government', not as some kind of milqutoast loonie leftie 'sharing is caring', or even as some 'drain the swamp' tear-it-all-down exposure, but as an engine for social and economic development. It's not 'just for research'. It's not 'just to give people warm fuzzies'. It's about money, 'our' money, who's giving it, whos getting it, how it's being spent and what can people do with the fruit of that spend, _including_ making money off that fruit. 

If you wanted to gently glaze an economic policy with soft and fluffies, but still throw all your money into private sector technical and economic gains, this is a pretty spiffy way to Open-Wash it, and _still_ get something socially valuable out of it even as a raging capitalist. 

Government, give data for 'free', I will sell services to your population on top of it efficiently, as long as efficiently includes my profits. Neoliberal Nirvana.

And the "Great Britain" shot to the top stop in the 'Global Open Data Index' according to the [Open Knowledge Foundation](https://okfn.org/en/)
